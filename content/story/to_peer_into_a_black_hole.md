# To Peer Into a Black Hole



To peer into a black hole.
Those dark, unassailable vaults scattered across the universe, slinging with them a myriad of stars and uncountable worlds, must surely hold the secrets of the cosmos.

To reach in and take a secret from its depths would be an impossible achievement.
But such a challenge is often the focus of humankind, just as it was with the Light's End Laboratories and their chief astrophysicist, Dr. Olben.

He paced the floor of his temporary office, a small corner room in the Houston Space Center in Texas.
He had arrived from the United Kingdom just a few hours ago, and jet lag tugged at him.
He was tired, sure, but the adrenaline that ran through his bloodstream kept him fully and completely alert.
Hands shaking, forehead sweating, he stepped out into the hall and approached the mission control center.

In thirty three minutes, NASA was expecting communication to arrive from Pandora-7.
It had been traveling for over twenty years on its way to the center of the Milky Way.
Ten years ago, it reached the black hole at the center of the galaxy, Sagittarius A.

The plan had been relatively simple for such a historic endeavor, and Dr. Olben's mathematics made it seemingly possible.
Through an experimental wormhole, the craft would arrive just one light-year from the black hole in only three years of travel.
Then, over the next two years, it would approach the black hole and dip into the event horizon.

Using a technique devised by Dr. Olben and his lab -- one that would take far too long to explain -- the craft would read as much data as it could from the black hole and fire it off into space.
Encoded in the Hawking radiation that Sagittarius A was constantly spewing, the data would travel safely away from the long arm of the black hole's gravity.

It would take another ten years from that moment for the radiation to travel through the wormhole and back to Earth, but the time had finally come.
Dr. Olben, twenty years older than his ambitious young self, was about to see the result of his life's work.
The secrets of Sagittarius A were about to be uncovered.

Olben sat down in the mission control center, where scientists and engineers were checking instrumentation, calibrating sensors, and making final adjustments.
Hawking radiation is difficult to detect, and this had become a worldwide effort.
Scientific institutions in the Americas, across Europe and Asia, and in the southern hemisphere across Africa and New Zealand, everyone had rigged up radiation sinks to guarantee that *somebody* would pick up the data.

"You ready?
Twenty seven minutes."
Dr. Camille, NASA's director of engineering, sat down next to him.
He met her eyes and smiled, portraying what he hoped was a calm demeanor, despite the rush he was feeling.

"I think so.
But, hey, it's been twenty years -- I don't mind waiting another half hour."

She grinned and held out her hand.
"Congratulations.
No matter what we get from that probe, this is an incredible achievement."
Olben shook her hand earnestly.

More time passed, and nineteen minutes remained.
Dr. Olben was fixed to his seat, next to the engineers who would be responsible for aggregating the received data across all the world's Hawking radiation sensors.
Their eyes didn't leave their screens for a second, not even to acknowledge Olben's presence.
Twenty years was a long time to prepare for something.

Thirteen minutes.
NASA's broadcasting of the event on the internet had gone live.
Just a minute ago, Dr. Olben gave a sixty second interview with news anchors from all over the world.
It was over with quickly enough, and he returned to his seat.

Six minutes.
The adrenaline was widespread.
Legs bounced anxiously in their chairs, pencils tapped, and fingernails were chewed aggressively.
The Hawking radiation engineers still fixed their eyes on the screens in front of them, monitoring every available metric to ensure all equipment was operating as expected.

Two minutes.
A quiet fell over the room as Dr. Olben and the engineers watched the screen.
Olben was sweating.
So were the engineers.

Thirty seconds.
Nobody knew what the data would be, but the world expected something amazing.
More likely, though, it would be gibberish; raw radiation that would be painstakingly converted to binary and processed for years to come.
Dr. Olben was well aware that he would likely die before any sense was made of it.

Ten seconds.
The equipment was functioning perfectly.
Pandora-7's data was hurtling through the inner solar system.
At 1.3 seconds, it flew passed the moon.

Zero.
Data streamed in over the engineers' screens.
"We are receiving!" One confirmed.

A few seconds passed, as the secrets of Sagittarius A were etched into hard drives across the planet.
The collection went quickly; as soon as it had begun, it ceased.

"Hawking radiation received successfully from Pandora-7.
Beginning translation."

Dr. Olben watched as the data was converted to binary.
He narrowed his eyes as the first few bytes were squeezed out of the radiation.

"That's strange," he said over an engineer's shoulder, "that first byte looks like an ASCII encoding."
Strange indeed, but the engineer confirmed it.
The first byte received was "01000011," the ASCII encoding of the English letter "C."

"Probably just a coincidence," said the engineer.
Then the next byte came in.

This one was "01101111" -- another ASCII encoding.
The lowercase letter "o."
Dr. Olben exchanged a glance with the engineer.

Slowly the bytes came in as the world's best supercomputers collectively chugged along to translate them.
'Co.'

'Con.'

'Cong.'

Five minutes had passed, and a first word had been translated: "Congratulations."
A feeling of dread crept over Dr. Olben, though he didn't know why.

Silence filled the room as the bytes kept coming in.
'Y.'

'Yo.'

'You.'

*Is this some sort of trick?* thought Dr. Olben.
*No way in hell the data from a black hole would translate perfectly to an English word.*

'You h.'

'You ha.'

'You hav.'

'You have.'

What did it mean?

At last, the final word came through, and the message was laid bare on the screens across the mission control center.
'Congratulations. You have won.'

Silence.
Nobody knew what to think.
Nobody knew what to say.

Then, outrage.
Arguments broke out among the engineers and the scientists in the room.
Dr. Camille met Dr. Olben's eyes from across the room, and they exchanged confused glances.
At last, the Hawking radiation engineers looked away from their screens as they engaged in the debates.

Surely this was some sick joke, right?
Somebody had hacked into NASA and injected those letters over top of the *real* data.
But who?
Why?
*Was my work all for nothing?* fumed Dr. Olben.
The dread still hung above his neck, but he still didn't know why.

"Wait -- WAIT!" shouted one of the Hawking radiation engineers.
"There's more data coming through!"
The room fell silent again.

More letters.
English ASCII character encodings, despite every possible sequence of bytes in existence, continued to stream in.

'R.'

'Re.'

'Res.'

'Rest.'

Dr. Olben watched, unable to look away, as the message from deep within Sagittarius A presented itself.

'Restarting in 10 years, 1 minute.'

It was ten years to the day since that data was sent.
It had traveled ten light years.
That meant...

Dr. Osben stumbled backwards and fell into his chair.
The dread was overwhelming.
Ten years, and *one minute*?

It had already been thirty seconds since the data was translated.
Everyone was looking at him.
He found Dr. Camille's eyes again.
There were tears.
She was terrified.

Fifty seconds had passed.
Dr. Olben closed his eyes and felt for his wedding ring.
Then he took a deep breath.
