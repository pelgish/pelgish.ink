# Beneath the Frozen Haze





Titan is no place for a human, but equally so is it no match for human determination.
Yet the burning purpose that brought Cindil here had been worn down by endless routine; she felt degraded, like a stone smoothed by water.

Weak sunlight drifted through the haze onto the tawny landscape.
It was cold -- so impossibly cold that the crust they stood on was almost entirely water, frozen solid.
She hated this place.



Her crewmates -- the only other humans within a thousand miles -- didn't mind so much.
On mornings like these,  they would linger for a while, gazing up at the familiar sun, their dark atmosuits against the pale ice-rock.
Only today, the haze was especially dense, and the sun was nowhere to be found.

Cindil sealed a final panel on the hull and knocked it with her fist.
"All set," she said.
"Let's go diving."

Gants turned away from the morning light and sighed.
"Thickest haze yet.
I've never seen the sun completely gone like that."

"We'll see it next time," Cindil offered, though her indifference was clear.
Gants nodded and spoke no more, following Cindil into the gunship.
Hilma, the mission commander, and Lorn, the pilot, entered behind them.

"We're going in a new direction today," began Hilma, "but we're going to have to blast a new tunnel.
The scanners show a wide opening to the southeast.
This'll be a good spot for the new pump."

"How much pipe to connect it to the main line?" asked Cindil.

"About half a mile.
Should take three full dives to finish.
The old pump is failing; we need this to stabilize the water supply."

Cindil nodded, smiling to herself.
Most days blurred together -- no change, no comforts of home.
But the subsurface dives?
What is danger, if not the thrill of being human?

Gants double-checked the seal on the ship's door.
"Let's be careful, team," he said, peering out the window one more time.



"Don't worry," grinned Lorn, reaching for the controls.
The ship roared to life.
"You're flying Lorn Airways.
Expect mild turbulence as we blast a new path through the ice, but we'll go down and come back up in perfect safety."
The ship tipped downward and submerged itself into the methane lake upon which it had been docked.


For three hours the gunship blasted holes through miles of ice-rock.
Each blast drew them deeper into the moon, until at last liquid methane gave way to the brackish, murky water of the subsurface ocean.

Cindil watched the water slosh against the window.
The depression that dampened her mind began to wash away -- some always remained -- but she felt herself again.
She smiled.

At the bottom, Lorn blasted the last chunks away and set them down on a wide ridge.
Cindil and Hilma prepared their swimsuits -- great bulky casings to withstand Titan's pressurized ocean.
With help from Gants and Lorn, the two divers stepped through the waterlock and entered the great murky dark.

Cindil relished the ocean blanketing her on all sides.
It certainly wasn't the Caribbean, but it felt a little like home.
Though, this time, the light from her helmet seemed to fall short.
The murk was thicker today, just like the haze in the sky.

An hour of uneventful work passed as Cindil and Hilma installed the pump and ten sections of pipe.
Lorn's voice came as a surprise after a long time of silence.

"Cindil, Hilma, I'm seeing some strange readings on the sonar.
I think the sensor is blocked -- can you check for salt buildup?"

"On it," answered Hilma.
She held up a fist to Cindil -- *halt work* -- and went to wipe the sonar clean.

"No salt," she reported.
"What are you seeing?"

"It's showing a big mass moving across our nose," Lorn said, "about a quarter-mile away."

"Moving?" asked Gants.
"Maybe it's just phantom data?"

"Yeah, probably an issue with the computer," Lorn agreed.
"We can fix it once we've surfaced."

Hilma gave him a thumbs-up from outside the window.
"For now, try firing a sonar blast at it; that should clear it up."

High-pitched pings echoed into the darkness.
Cindil swam to the front of the ship and gazed into the unknown.

"Well," Lorn chuckled, "I guess it didn't like that because it's heading towards us now."
Gants felt a knot in his stomach but said nothing.

Cindil scanned the utter dark.
Was there something out there?
Her eyes were playing tricks on her.

"Okay," answered Hilma, "just ignore it for now."
Her voice wavered.
"Let's--"

Without warning, a deep hum vibrated across the water.
It reverberated off the swimsuits and the ship's hull, magnifying into a deafening wail.
Cindil could see Hilma's mouth moving, but she couldn't hear a thing.

For a moment it ceased, and through ringing ears she caught her crewmate's voice.

"Get back--"

The terrible sound came again, crashing on them like a wave.

"--the ship!
Get back to the ship!"
It was Gants' voice.

Just as Cindil registered the words, a blinding light exploded out from the darkness, saturating the gunship in a pale orange.
Gants had been throwing desperate hand signals through the window, but he stopped, arms frozen and eyes wide.
She spun around.

The waters were still murky, but the peril was clear.
Before her was a massive structure, taller and wider than her eyes could discern.
Huge, jagged limbs, like the roots of a mangrove, drove themselves into the ocean floor and stretched over the gunship.
An orange eye of pure light gazed out at Cindil.
Terrified, she met its gaze.
There was a flash, a great boom, and the diver was gone.


There, in the depths of the ocean, on a strange moon in the outer reaches of the solar system, was our first warning.
They were here first.
